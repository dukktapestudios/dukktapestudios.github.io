The whole path

Dukktape Studios publishes small games on this site. Rooster Rumble is a rooster battle card game with a public rules page and a card list. Use it as a picture of a simple game page. It is an example, and your costs will be your own.

Twelve steps, from a locked ruleset to a second look at the numbers. A longer publish walkthrough lives at ShuffleKit (opens in a new tab).

Jump to the next step

  1. 1Lock the game

    Finish the rules and the card list. Run a blind playtest: strangers, and the rulebook only. Fix the game until they can finish with you out of the room. Polish the art after the game works.

    Then stop.

  2. 2Build a real prototype

    Sleeve a home print, or order one print-on-demand proof. Shops people use for a first proof include The Game Crafter (opens in a new tab), MakePlayingCards, and DriveThruCards. Take photos. You will want them when you sell.

    Then stop.

  3. 3Finish print-ready art and rules

    Use the printer's template. Export at 300 dpi. Include bleed and trim. Outline the fonts. Size the rules for the box. Count the cards before you choose a tuck box.

    Then stop.

  4. 4Protect what you can

    Copyright covers your art, your rulebook text, and your layout. The idea of a card game, and the way the rules play, sit outside copyright. Registering with Copyright.gov (opens in a new tab) is optional. It can help if you later need to enforce the right.

    A trademark can cover the name and the logo. Search the USPTO trademark basics (opens in a new tab) before you print boxes. Games are often filed in Class 28.

    Then stop.

  5. 5Put sales under a real business

    Before money moves, use an LLC or a sole proprietorship, plus a bank account that is separate from personal spending. Track income and expenses. Ask a tax pro once sales start. This page leaves the choice of business form to you and that pro.

    Then stop.

  6. 6Pick one sell route

    Print on demand tests demand. A crowdfunding campaign can pay for a print run. Savings can pay for a print run when demand is already real. A publisher pitch hands the operations to someone else. First games usually start with print on demand.

    Then stop.

  7. 7Get quotes and set a price

    For print on demand, the price is the print cost plus the shop's royalty. For a print run, send the same spec to three printers. Add freight, duties, storage, and fulfillment.

    Public guides often plan retail at about 3 to 5 times the landed cost of one copy, after fees. Build your price from your quote.

    Then stop.

  8. 8Make a home page for buyers

    Show photos, a rules PDF, a short how-to-play clip, and a way to buy or join a Notify Me list. A simple page is enough. Rooster Rumble is one public game page on this site.

    Then stop.

  9. 9Build a tiny audience first

    Start a mailing list, a Discord, game nights, and a few reviewer prototypes. Crowdfunding usually wants about 6 to 12 months of this work before launch day. Print on demand can sell while the list is still small.

    Then stop.

  10. 10Launch sales

    Print on demand: publish the listing and tell the list. Crowdfunding: pre-launch, then the live campaign, then a pledge manager. Print run: approve the proofs, manufacture, then fulfill. Kickstarter's tabletop launch guide (opens in a new tab) is the crowdfunding version of this step.

    Then stop.

  11. 11Fulfill the orders

    Plan packaging, carriers, and customs. Pad the calendar. Ship a test box to yourself before you ship to anyone else. On a crowdfunding campaign, put manufacturing and shipping inside the goal. The goal is the smallest run you can deliver, plus freight, fulfillment, fees, and a cushion.

    Read Kickstarter's fulfillment handbook (opens in a new tab) before you promise a date.

    Then stop.

  12. 12Iterate after real sales

    Cut waste. Change the price if demand holds. Make an expansion when the base game is selling. Log the real unit cost before you spend on the next print.

    Then stop.

Sell routes

Pick one route for this game. You can change routes on the next game.

Four ways to sell
Route Money down Cost per copy Fits when
Print on demand About a proof, often $10 or more High First game, demand unknown
Crowdfund, then print Marketing plus a prototype Low, because backers fund the run You can build an audience
Print run from savings Thousands up front Lowest Demand is already proven, or this is a later game
Pitch a publisher Time plus a prototype The publisher keeps most of the margin You want someone else to handle manufacturing and sales

Print on demand

You upload the files once. The shop prints a copy when someone orders it. Public notes on shops such as The Game Crafter and DriveThruCards often leave the maker about 70% of the amount above the print cost. Open the shop's current royalty page and run your own price. Match the template, order a proof, set retail, then publish.

Crowdfund, then print

Kickstarter's public fees have been about 5%, plus payment processing around 3% and $0.30 per pledge. Gamefound sits in a similar range. A working budget often uses about 8% for platform fees and 10 to 20% of the goal for marketing. Read the live fee page. The goal covers the smallest deliverable run, freight, fulfillment, fees, and a contingency. Build the audience for about 6 to 12 months first. After funding: pledge manager, a physical proof, the print run, then shipping.

Print run

Offset printing often starts around 500 to 1,000 copies. Names that show up in public manufacture guides include PrintNinja, Panda, Delano, and Shuffled Ink. One public planning example is about $5 to $7 a copy at 500 copies for a 54-card deck plus a tuck box, using PrintNinja-style published numbers. Get your quote. Proof to pallet is often 2 to 3 months. Send the same spec to three printers, order a physical proof, and decide where the boxes will live.

Two public write-ups: Chicago Game and Card on manufacturing (opens in a new tab), and The Game Crafter's note on long-run printers (opens in a new tab).

Pitch a publisher

You spend time and a solid prototype. The publisher takes on manufacturing and sales, and keeps most of the margin. Choose this when you want the operations off your desk.

Money math

Planning ranges from public 2026 guides. Each line is a place to start a quote. Your invoice replaces the range.

  • A print-on-demand proof is often about the cost of a takeout meal. Simple decks on public shop pages are often about $9 to $20.
  • A common print-on-demand split leaves the maker about 70% of the margin above the print cost. Confirm it in that shop's calculator.
  • A bulk run of 500 to 1,000 copies is often planned around $2,000 to $6,000 before freight and tariffs. The spread is wide.
  • Crowdfunding fees are often budgeted near 8%, with marketing near 10 to 20% of the goal.
  • A UPC or GTIN from GS1 US has been about $30 for a single barcode when shops will stock the game. A pure print-on-demand listing, or a crowdfunding-only release, can wait on the barcode until a store asks. Check the current GS1 US price.

Legal, in plain language

  • Copyright covers art, text, and layout. It leaves out the bare idea and the how-to-play. See the Copyright.gov games page (opens in a new tab).
  • A trademark covers the name and the logo. Search before the boxes are printed. USPTO Class 28 is the usual class for games. Start at the USPTO basics page (opens in a new tab).
  • Patents are rare for card games, and they are expensive.
  • Safety marks and age labels depend on the country. Settle them before the press run.
  • Use a written contract with each artist, manufacturer, and fulfillment partner.

After the first sale

Write the FAQ once. Ask a buyer for a photo or a short review. Log unit cost, shipping, and returns. After those numbers are real, look at an expansion or a better print through crowdfunding.

Focus tricks

  • This week: order one paid print-on-demand proof of the deck that is closest to done.
  • One sticky note holds one step title.
  • Run the blind playtest before you polish the art.
  • If a large print bill makes you anxious, stay on print on demand until more than 20 strangers have bought a copy.
  • A faceless brand is fine. Sell the cards.

01What AI can and can't do

The short version

Today's AI is software that learned from huge amounts of writing, images and sound. It is very good at working with language and patterns: writing, summarizing, explaining, translating, brainstorming, making pictures and music, and writing code. It is not a person, it does not always know when it is wrong, and it works best when you check its work.

What it does well

  • Writes and edits: Microsoft's Copilot works inside Word, Excel, PowerPoint and Outlook, and Google's Gemini works inside Gmail and Docs.
  • Answers questions with sources: Perplexity shows the web pages behind each answer so you can click and check.
  • Helps on your phone and at home: Apple's Siri AI (beta, iOS 27) can use your personal context, what is on screen and app actions. Amazon's Alexa+ is a home assistant.
  • Makes pictures, songs, voices and short video clips from a description, using tools like GPT Image, Suno, ElevenLabs and Veo.
  • Writes and fixes code: coding tools such as Claude Code and OpenAI Codex can work on a software project, and Anthropic says its Fable 5.2 can run engineering work for days (the company's own claim).
  • Does chores in real apps: xAI says teams inside its own company use Grok Bot agents to update a sales database with call notes and to process invoices that arrive in Gmail.

Where it falls short

  • It can be confidently wrong. This is a well-known limit of today's AI, so double-check anything that matters. OpenAI even updated GPT-6 in ChatGPT on October 29, 2026 to reduce “overly flattering” answers, a sign that AI can lean toward telling you what you want to hear.
  • It does not know recent events unless it searches the web. Claude Fable 5.2's knowledge ends around June 2026, and Grok 4.7's around May 2026.
  • Its memory is limited. Each conversation holds only so much, and the model does not automatically remember you in the way a friend does, unless the product has a memory feature turned on.
  • It is not all-in-one. Fable 5.2, for example, can read images but only answers in text. Video clips from the leading tools are short, and OpenAI shut down its Sora video tool in 2026.
  • It has usage limits and costs. The strongest models use up a paid plan's allowance faster, so companies recommend a smaller everyday model for most tasks.
  • Agents that act on your behalf can make bigger mistakes. Good ones ask for approval first, and you should keep a person in charge of anything that sends messages, spends money or deletes things.

03Words you'll hear

The jargon sounds harder than the ideas. These nine words cover most of what you will run into.

AI model

The trained program at the center

A model is software that learned patterns from huge amounts of text, images and sound, and uses them to produce answers. Grok, GPT, Claude and Gemini are all models. Apps like ChatGPT are built around them.

Chatbot

You type, it answers

A chatbot is a conversation. You ask, it replies, and you steer. The question you type is called a prompt.

Agent

It does tasks, not just talk

An agent is an AI that takes a goal and works through the steps using tools, such as browsing, running code or using apps. Good ones ask your approval before important steps.

Context window

How much it can keep in mind at once

This is the model's working memory for one conversation. Fable 5.2 can hold 1 million tokens and Grok 4.7 500 thousand. A token is a small chunk of text, a bit shorter than a typical word. Bigger lets you hand over longer documents.

Reasoning model

It thinks before it answers

These models work through a problem in steps before replying. Answers on hard tasks are often better, but they are slower and use more of your plan's allowance.

Multimodal

Works with more than text

A multimodal model can understand pictures or audio as well as words. Making pictures, songs and video is usually done by separate, specialized models.

Open vs closed

Can you download it?

Closed models (Claude, GPT, Gemini, Grok) run only on the company's computers. Open-weight models (DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, Qwen3.8, Meta's Muse Spark Open) can be downloaded and run yourself. Each comes with its own license.

Cloud vs local

Whose computer does the work

Cloud means the company's computers do it: strongest and easiest, and your questions go to the company. Local means your own computer does it: private, but limited by your hardware.

Hallucination

A confident wrong answer

AI can state something false in a calm, convincing way. This is a well-known limit of today's AI, not just of one product, which is why checking matters.

06In the news: Fable 5.2

You may have heard about “Fable 5.2”. It is real: Claude Fable 5.2, a model from Anthropic, released November 9, 2026.

What it is
Anthropic's most capable model, built for the hardest technical work: big coding projects, deep research, finance and agents that work on their own for hours
Price for developers
$8 per million input tokens and $40 per million output tokens (official docs)
Who can use it
Paid Claude plans (on the Pro plan it runs on usage credits; Max and Ultra include it up to half the weekly limit) and developers through the API
Is it the one to pick?
Not for most people. Anthropic itself says to start with Claude Opus 5.5 for everyday work
What people say
Third-party roundups of posts on X† describe big excitement about the demos, then a more mixed view that Opus 5.5 is the better daily tool because Fable costs more and uses limits faster

It is a good example of how this field works: the newest, biggest model gets the headlines, while a cheaper model often does the daily job. The benchmark numbers Anthropic published are its own tests.

07Staying safe

These are general good habits, not from any one source.

  • Don't type passwords, account numbers, ID numbers or other private details into a chatbot.
  • Check the privacy and data settings of any tool before sharing anything sensitive.
  • Verify important facts, especially about health, money, law or safety, with an official source or a professional.
  • Be careful with anything that asks you to pay, sign up quickly or install something because “AI said so.”
  • Be skeptical of voices, photos and videos online. AI can make convincing fakes.

0820 questions to keep learning

If you don't know what to ask, start here. Copy a block into any assistant and swap the parts in [brackets] for your own details. The last question is the most useful one.

Learn the basics

1. Explain what AI is as if I've never used a computer for anything but email. Use 5 short paragraphs, then quiz me with 3 questions.
2. What is the difference between a chatbot and an AI agent? Give me one real example of each.
3. What kinds of things are you good at, and what kinds of things are you bad at?
4. How do you know things, and what don't you know about?
5. What does it mean when AI is wrong with confidence, and how can I catch it?

Choose the right tool

6. I want to [your goal]. Which AI tools could help, and which is the easiest to start with? Free options first.
7. What's the difference between the free and paid version of [tool], and is it worth paying for someone like me?
8. Using only official pages, what does [tool] cost today? Give me the date you checked and the links.
9. What can AI NOT do reliably for [your goal]? Where will I still need to do the work myself?
10. Is it safe to share [type of information] with an AI tool? What should I never share?

Get real work done

11. Turn this rough idea into a one-page plan with the next three small steps: [paste idea]
12. Read my draft and tell me the three weakest parts first, then what works: [paste draft]
13. I want to [learn, make or fix something]. Ask me what you need to know, then give me step-by-step instructions and tell me what to double-check.
14. Rewrite this message in a friendlier, shorter tone, and give me two versions: [paste message]
15. Teach me [topic] in 7 days at 20 minutes a day, with one tiny exercise each day.

Check and stay current

16. What is the strongest argument that your last answer is wrong? What would I need to verify?
17. List every claim in this article and mark each one as sourced, unsourced or hype: [paste article]
18. What changed in AI in the last 30 days that matters for [my situation]? Cite sources with dates.
19. Before you explain [topic], ask me three questions to find out what I already know.
20. What should I be asking you that I haven't thought to ask?

09Is it hype or real?

This field moves monthly and many people are selling something. Run any big claim through these seven checks.

  1. Who is saying it, and what do they gain?

    A company's launch post is real information about its product, but it is also an ad.

  2. Who ran the benchmark?

    Companies choose which tests and rivals to show. Even Anthropic's own table shows Google's Gemini 4 Carbon beating Fable 5.2 on one business-workflow test (53.9% vs 51.4%).

  3. What is the date?

    A ranking from two months ago may already be out of date. Look for dates on everything.

  4. Is it a demo or daily use?

    Reviewers said Fable 5.2 wins the demos while Opus 5.5 keeps the day job. Ask what it is like on ordinary tasks.

  5. What does a finished task cost?

    Compare the cost of finishing a job, not the headline price. A pricier model that gets it right the first time can beat a cheap one that needs five tries.

  6. Can you test it yourself in ten minutes?

    Give two tools the same real task from your own life. Your result beats any chart.

  7. Does it promise too much?

    “Replaces every job”, “guaranteed income” or “never wrong” are signs to slow down. Ask for sources and open them.

10A simple 7-day learning path

Twenty to thirty minutes a day is plenty. If a day is too much, skip it and keep going.

  1. Day 1: Learn the words

    Pick one free assistant (ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude or Grok). Ask questions 1 to 5 and write down three ideas that surprised you.

  2. Day 2: Try a second assistant

    Give the same simple task, such as explaining a topic or planning a week of meals, to two different free assistants. Compare them.

  3. Day 3: Use it on a real chore

    Have it rewrite an email, summarize an article or plan a small project. Use questions 11 to 14. Then check the result yourself.

  4. Day 4: Learn something new

    Use question 15 to start a 7-day mini-lesson on a topic you're curious about.

  5. Day 5: Try something creative

    Make a picture or a short song from a description with a free tool. Read the tool's rules on how you may use what it makes.

  6. Day 6: Try research with sources

    Ask a research tool such as Perplexity a real question. Open two of the links it cites and see whether they say what it claims.

  7. Day 7: Review and decide

    Ask questions 18 and 20. Note what saved time, what was wrong, and what you'd pay for. Choose one thing to go deeper on next week.

01What a PCB is

A printed circuit board (PCB) is a flat board that holds electronic parts and connects them with thin copper paths, so you do not need loose wires. Nearly every gadget you own has one. You can read a short overview in SparkFun’s PCB Basics.

Think of it like a tiny road map. The parts are the towns. The copper traces are the roads. Everything else on the board is there to protect the roads and label the towns.

The parts of the board

Copper traces
The thin copper lines that carry signals and power between parts. Wider traces carry more current.
Pads
Small exposed copper spots where you solder a part’s legs or contacts.
Vias
Tiny plated holes that connect copper on one layer to copper on another, like a stairwell between floors.
Solder mask
The coating, often green, that covers the copper so solder only sticks to the pads and stray touches do not short things out.
Silkscreen
The printed ink labels: part names, pin numbers, polarity marks, your logo.

How many layers: 1, 2, or 4

Layer counts at a glance
LayersWhat it isGood for
1Copper on one side only.Very simple boards with a few through-hole parts. Cheap, but routing gets cramped fast.
2Copper on top and bottom, joined by vias.The usual starting point for hobby and learning boards, including most small microcontroller projects.
4Two outer copper layers plus two inner layers, often one for ground and one for power.Denser or faster designs and radio parts. Espressif recommends a four-layer board for the ESP32-S3, and says a two-layer design can also be used with care.

SMD vs through-hole

Through-hole (THT)

Parts have wire legs that pass through holes and get soldered on the other side. They are bigger and sturdier, and much easier to solder by hand. Good for connectors, buttons, and your very first board.

Surface-mount (SMD)

Parts sit flat on pads on the surface. They are small, cheaper at volume, and what machines place. Hand soldering gets harder as parts shrink; larger sizes (such as 0805) are friendlier than tiny ones.

02Why design your own

You do not need a custom board to learn electronics. A breadboard is perfect for trying ideas. A custom board is what you reach for when the idea works and you want it to keep working in a smaller, tidier form.

Four ways to build the same circuit
OptionStrengthTrade-off
BreadboardNo soldering, instant changes. Best for experiments.Loose wires can wiggle out. Not meant to live in a finished project.
PerfboardSoldered and permanent, cheap, good for one-offs.Hand wiring is slow and easy to get wrong. Hard to make a second identical copy.
Off-the-shelf dev boardFast, well tested, plenty of community help.Fixed size and shape, with parts and pins you may not need.
Custom PCBExact size and shape, tidy and repeatable, and every copy is the same.Takes time to design, costs a little to order, and mistakes mean waiting for a new batch.

Size and shape

A custom board can be the exact size and outline your enclosure needs, with no unused parts.

Reliability

Soldered traces do not shake loose the way jumper wires can.

Cost at volume

One board costs a few dollars to a few tens of dollars to make. Making many copies spreads the setup work, but the exact math varies by fab and parts.

Products you can sell

If you ever want to sell or share a device, a repeatable board is the starting point. Selling hardware has its own rules, such as safety and radio certification, which are outside this guide.

And one more honest reason: learning. Designing a board teaches you how circuits really fit together, which makes you better at every other part of electronics.

03Real goals

A good first reason to make a board is to turn something that already works on a breadboard into a clean, repeatable thing. Three generic examples:

Example 1

A microcontroller alarm

An ESP32-based door or motion alarm that works on a breadboard with a few jumper wires. On a custom board, the microcontroller, a buzzer, a sensor connector, and a power input all sit in a small enclosure with solid connections.

Example 2

A sensor board

A small board that carries a temperature or light sensor and breaks out four neat pins (power, ground, and two signals) so it can be reused on other projects.

Example 3

A robot controller

A board that combines a microcontroller, motor driver, power regulator, and labeled connectors for motors and sensors, replacing a tangle of separate modules and wires.

04The workflow, step by step

The tools differ, but the path is nearly the same everywhere. KiCad’s Getting Started guide walks the same order in detail.

  1. 1. Idea

    Write down what the board must do, what power it uses, how big it can be, and how you will connect to it. A few plain sentences are enough.

  2. 2. Schematic

    Draw the circuit as symbols and lines, like a wiring diagram. You are saying what connects to what, not where things sit yet. Run the ERC (electrical rules check) to catch things like unconnected pins.

  3. 3. Parts, symbols, and footprints

    Each part needs a symbol (its picture in the schematic) and a footprint (the pad pattern on the board). A wrong footprint means a part that will not fit, so check it against the part’s datasheet. If you plan to have a fab place parts, pick ones the fab stocks (for example, from JLCPCB’s parts library).

  4. 4. Layout

    Set the board outline, place the parts, and route the copper traces. Place parts first, then route. Power and ground deserve extra care.

  5. 5. Design rule check (DRC)

    The DRC compares your layout to the fab’s minimum rules, such as trace width, spacing, and drill size. Get the rules from your chosen fab first (see the learning path) and fix every error or understand why it is safe to keep.

  6. 6. Output files

    Export Gerbers (the layer-by-layer manufacturing pictures) and drill files. For assembly, also export a BOM (bill of materials: the shopping list) and a pick-and-place (also called placement or CPL) file that says where each part goes. JLCPCB’s KiCad export guide and OSH Park’s KiCad guide show the steps.

  7. 7. Order from a fab

    Upload the files and read the instant quote and preview before you pay. Popular hobby fabs include JLCPCB, PCBWay, and OSH Park. Ordering is your decision; this guide never asks you to buy.

  8. 8. Assembly

    Hand soldering: cheaper and a great skill, best with through-hole or larger SMD parts. Fab assembly: the fab places and solders the parts for you, which is neater for small SMD parts but adds setup and part costs (see the cost section).

  9. 9. Bring-up and testing

    Do not plug everything in at once. Check for shorts between power and ground with a multimeter before applying power. Then power up gently, check voltages, and bring each part to life one at a time. Test points help here (see the mistakes section).

Expect a first board to need a second try. That is normal, and it is part of learning. Small first boards keep the second try cheap.

05Free tools, and AI-assisted tools

The two free classics

Free PCB design tools
ToolWhat it isBeginner note
KiCad A cross-platform, open-source PCB design suite with a schematic editor, PCB editor, 3D viewer, and built-in rules checks. Its site lists version 10.0 as current in November 2026. Runs on your own computer. Very widely used, with official docs and a huge community. A solid long-term choice.
EasyEDA A free PCB design tool that runs in the browser, with a downloadable desktop app also offered. Its site links straight to JLCPCB for ordering. Quick to start, with no install needed. Handy if you plan to order from JLCPCB. Your projects live in an online account.

Newer AI-assisted tools, honestly framed

A newer group of tools lets you describe a board in plain language and gives you a first draft. Two examples, described only as their own sites describe them:

  • Flux says it can turn an idea into a schematic, a routed PCB, and fab-ready files with AI, right in your browser.
  • ProtoFlow says its AI picks real parts, draws the schematic and routes the PCB, and that you review it and export Gerbers. Its home page shows one prompt producing an ESP32 breakout board in a 30-minute run (sped up in the video), shown as ERC and DRC clean. It says it opens KiCad, Altium, and EAGLE projects and has a free plan with no card and no expiry. At the time of writing, its page shows a Mac download.

What this means for you. These tools can be a useful way to get a first draft, find parts, and see what a finished design might look like. But a human still has to review the schematic, check every footprint against datasheets, and make sure the DRC result is meaningful. Even ProtoFlow’s own page says you review the draft and you sign off on the board that ships. A “clean” DRC only means the board follows the rules that were checked. It does not prove the circuit does what you want.

They are also not a substitute for learning the basics. If you cannot read a schematic, you cannot tell when the draft is wrong. Learn the steps in this guide first, then use AI as a helper. Plans, limits, and features of these tools change often, so check their sites yourself.

06A first project, with real costs

Suggested first board

Make it boring on purpose. A good first board is a small two-layer breakout or LED board: a microcontroller header or small module, a power input, one LED with a resistor, a button, and a few labeled pins. Pick through-hole or larger SMD parts so you can solder it yourself. Keep it around the size of a postage stamp to a business card.

When that goes well, a natural next step is a board built around an ESP32-S3 module (the chip, flash, and antenna on a small pre-built board) rather than the bare chip, which keeps the hardest radio layout off your plate. Espressif’s guidelines use a module as their layout example and explain how to place it on a base board.

07Common beginner mistakes

Parts

Wrong footprint

The most common one. Check each footprint’s pads against the part’s datasheet, and print the layout at 100% size to hold the real part against it.

Parts

Not checking the BOM and stock

A part that is out of stock at your fab can hold up the whole order. Check availability and pick common parts before you finalize the design.

Power

No decoupling capacitors

Chips need small capacitors close to their power pins to keep their supply steady. Espressif’s ESP32-S3 layout guidance calls for capacitors near the chip’s power pins and ground vias close to the capacitor ground pads.

Power

Trace width too small for the current

Thin traces get warm when they carry real current. Use a trace-width calculator (KiCad includes a PCB Calculator with a track width tool) and give power traces more room than signal traces. Espressif’s guide, for example, asks for main ESP32-S3 power traces to be no less than 25 mil wide.

Radio

No antenna keep-out

Copper, parts, or a metal case near a Wi-Fi antenna can ruin its range. Espressif’s guidance is to put a module’s PCB antenna outside the base board, or if it cannot overhang, to cut the base board away around it, and to leave generous clearance inside the final housing.

Testing

No test points

Add small exposed pads or headers for power, ground, and key signals. When something does not work on bring-up, you will be glad to have somewhere to put the probe.

Mechanical

Forgetting mounting holes

Plan how the board will be held in its enclosure, and leave room for screws and connectors that reach the edge.

Fab rules

Ignoring the fab’s rules

Every fab has minimum widths, spacing, and drill sizes, and they differ. For two-layer boards, for example, OSH Park lists 6 mil trace width and spacing as its minimum, while JLCPCB lists 4 mil for 1 oz copper on one- and two-layer boards. Load your fab’s rules into your DRC before you lay out the board.

Process

Skipping the review

Re-read the schematic against every datasheet, check the DRC result, and look at the fab’s preview before paying. A fresh set of eyes, or a good night’s sleep, catches mistakes.

08A learning path

  1. 1. Learn the vocabulary

    Read SparkFun’s PCB Basics to understand the parts of a board.

  2. 2. Follow KiCad’s official tutorial

    Install KiCad and work through Getting Started in KiCad. It takes you from a new project through the schematic, the board, design rule checking, and fabrication outputs. More options are on KiCad’s learning resources page.

  3. 3. Watch a full free walk-through

    A free, long KiCad PCB design tutorial (it uses an older KiCad 6 and an STM32 chip, so menus may look different, but the process is the same). The channel’s videos page lists more.

  4. 4. Read your fab’s design rules

    Before your first layout, read the rules: JLCPCB’s capabilities, PCBWay’s capabilities, and OSH Park’s 2-layer specs. Then follow a fab’s export guide, such as JLCPCB’s KiCad Gerber guide or OSH Park’s KiCad guide.

  5. 5. Learn the soldering side

    Adafruit’s Guide to Excellent Soldering and SparkFun’s through-hole soldering guide cover hand assembly.

  6. 6. When you reach an ESP32-S3 board

    Use Espressif’s ESP32-S3 hardware design guidelines, including the schematic checklist and the PCB layout design chapter. For ESP32 basics, see the ESP32 Crash Course on this site.

09Glossary

TermPlain meaning
BOMBill of materials: the list of every part on the board.
Bring-upThe careful first power-on and testing of a new board.
Decoupling capacitorA small capacitor near a chip’s power pin that keeps its supply steady.
DRCDesign rule check: software that tests your layout against manufacturing rules.
ERCElectrical rules check: software that tests your schematic for wiring mistakes.
FabThe factory that makes your board.
FootprintThe pad pattern on the board where a part is soldered.
GerberThe standard file format fabs use to make each layer of a board.
Keep-outAn area where copper or parts are not allowed, such as around an antenna.
Pick-and-place fileA file listing each part’s position and rotation, used by assembly machines. Also called CPL.
PadAn exposed copper spot where a part is soldered.
SchematicA diagram that shows how parts are connected, without showing where they sit.
SilkscreenThe printed labels on a board.
SMDSurface-mount device: a part that sits on top of the board.
Solder maskThe coating over copper that keeps solder only where you want it.
Test pointA pad or pin added so you can probe a signal.
THTThrough-hole technology: parts with legs that go through holes.
TraceA copper path that carries a signal or power.
ViaA plated hole that joins copper on different layers.

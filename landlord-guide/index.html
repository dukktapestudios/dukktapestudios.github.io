Landlord Crash Course

A plain-English path from “I might buy a rental” to the everyday work of keeping one safe, legal where you live, and understandable on a worksheet.

Education only. This is not legal advice, tax advice, or investment advice. It is not a rent to charge, a home value, a loan approval, or a promise of profit. Landlord-tenant rules are state and local. Read the official pages linked here, and talk with a qualified professional where you live before you buy, rent, repair, or evict.

1. Buying and financing

A rental is a house plus a job. The price is only one of the checks you write. This page separates those checks so the worksheet does not count the same dollar twice.

  • Purchase price is what you agree to pay for the property.
  • Down payment is the cash you put toward that price. The rest, if any, is the loan.
  • A cash purchase means the down payment equals the price. The loan payment is $0. Taxes, insurance, repairs, and vacancy can still cost money.
  • Interest and years decide the principal-and-interest payment. A zero-interest loan is paid as the loan divided by the number of months. A loan still needs a term of at least one year on this worksheet.
  • Closing costs, rehab, and other upfront cash are money you pay, but they are not the loan. They count as cash invested.

The loan line on the worksheet is principal and interest only. If a mortgage bill also holds an escrow for taxes and insurance, do not type that whole bill as the loan payment and also type the taxes and insurance. Type the principal and interest once, and type the taxes and insurance once in the operating costs.

This worksheet does not guess private mortgage insurance, points, or a rate buydown. If a lender quoted mortgage insurance, put that amount in the mortgage-insurance line. Leave it at zero if you do not have it. Do not hide it inside the interest rate and also type it as its own line.

If the lender rolls closing costs into the loan, do not count those dollars as both cash and debt. Lower the cash closing-cost line and reduce the down payment so the loan is larger by that amount.

A free or low-cost housing counselor can walk through a real mortgage with you. Start with the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau’s Owning a Home tools and the housing counselor search. Those sites are the source for current loan shopping steps. This page is not a lender.

2. Rental readiness

Before someone moves in, the place needs to be safe to live in and allowed to be a rental where it stands. There is no single national punch list that replaces your city or county.

  • Ask the city or county whether a rental license, registration, or inspection is required before occupancy.
  • Ask the building department whether recent electrical, plumbing, structural, or heating work needed a permit. Keep the records.
  • Ask an insurance agent whether a landlord policy fits, instead of a policy written for a home you live in. Flood insurance is a separate question. FEMA’s Flood Map Service Center is a lookup, not a decision this page can make.
  • “Habitable” is a legal word your state defines. Common ideas are heat, water, a working toilet, a weatherproof shell, and safe exits. Confirm the actual standard locally. Do not copy a temperature or a repair deadline from a different state.

Paint in most housing built before 1978 can be a lead hazard. EPA’s Lead-Based Paint Disclosure Rule says sellers and landlords of most of that housing must disclose known lead paint and hazards, share records, and give the pamphlet Protect Your Family From Lead in Your Home. The same page lists exemptions and says sellers must give buyers 10 days for a lead inspection or risk assessment unless they agree otherwise. Read the page before you rely on that sentence. Before anyone disturbs paint in a pre-1978 rental, read EPA’s Renovation, Repair and Painting rule. This course does not teach you how to scrape or sand that paint.

EPA’s radon pages explain testing. A result belongs to that house. Use EPA’s explanation of risk rather than a cutoff remembered from somewhere else.

3. Screening and fair housing

Write your criteria before you advertise. Apply that same written list to every applicant. Do not make up a new rule for one person.

HUD’s Fair Housing Act overview says the Act prohibits discrimination in housing because of:

  • Race
  • Color
  • National origin
  • Religion
  • Sex
  • Familial status
  • Disability

Read that HUD page for how those words are applied, including harassment. Many states and cities protect more groups, such as source of income, marital status, sexual orientation, or gender identity. Those lists are not the same everywhere. Check your state attorney general or a local fair housing agency before you write a screening rule.

Housing providers have duties around disability, including reasonable accommodations and reasonable modifications. HUD’s overview is the place to start. Do not deny a request from a script on this website.

If you use a paid background or credit report, the Fair Credit Reporting Act may apply to you and to the company that sold the report. Read the company’s instructions and the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau’s Regulation V materials. This page does not tell you which notice to hand an applicant.

People who believe they were discriminated against can report it to HUD. HUD’s report page lists online, phone, and mail options, including 1-800-669-9777. HUD’s investigation page says a Fair Housing Act allegation must be filed within one year of the last date of the alleged discrimination. That is HUD’s stated timing, and the page is the source.

4. The lease

Use a written lease. A handshake is hard to remember the same way six months later. A lease written for another state can include clauses your state forbids, or leave out clauses your state requires.

People usually need the lease to name the parties, the address, the start and end, the rent amount and due date, which utilities each side pays, how repairs are requested, how entry works, what the deposit is, and how the tenancy ends. Your state may require more and may ban some fees or waivers. Get a form meant for your state, or have a lawyer review one. This site does not offer a lease to copy.

Entry rules are local. Some places expect advance notice except in an emergency. The number of hours is not the same nationwide, so it is not printed here. Put the rule you confirmed into the lease, and follow it.

5. Deposits

There is no single U.S. security-deposit cap. A state, and sometimes a city, may limit the amount, require a separate account, require interest, and set a deadline to return the deposit with an itemized list of deductions. Those numbers change, and they are not guessed here.

Before you collect a deposit, read the landlord-tenant statute or your state attorney general’s landlord page. Keep the move-in condition notes you will need later if you claim a deduction. Do not spend the deposit as if it were already earned income.

A pet deposit, a holding fee, and the last month’s rent can be treated differently from a security deposit. Check which label your state uses before you invent a new fee.

6. During the tenancy

Reply to repair requests in writing, even when you also talk. A short dated note of what was reported and what you did next prevents a fight about memory.

Keep the relationship boring and consistent. Collect rent the way the lease says. Give receipts. Do not change the rules for one household because you are annoyed.

Many states limit retaliation, such as a sudden rent hike or a non-renewal right after a good-faith repair or code complaint. Whether that protection exists, and how long it lasts, is local. Look it up before you act. This page will not tell you how to evict someone. Eviction is a court process with its own forms and timelines.

If a household says they need a change because of a disability, pause and read HUD’s fair housing materials rather than answering from habit.

7. Maintenance, and what not to touch

You can look, test a button the device was built with, change a filter the manual says a resident may change, and write down what you see. The dangerous work belongs to licensed people.

  • Do not open an electrical panel, replace wiring, outlets, or breakers, or work on a live circuit. If a breaker trips again after the extra load is unplugged, call an electrician. Do not hold a breaker in the on position.
  • Do not repair gas lines, connectors, valves, or the inside of a furnace, boiler, or gas water heater. Do not relight equipment from instructions on this page.
  • If you smell gas, leave. Do not flip switches on the way out. Call the gas utility or emergency number from outside, and follow that utility’s card.
  • Do not scrape, sand, or demolish paint in a pre-1978 rental as a project from this course. Read EPA’s renovation rule first.
  • For mold, stop the water and read EPA’s Brief Guide to Mold, Moisture, and Your Home. That guide separates a small cleanup from a larger problem. This page does not give a cleanup recipe. Sewage, a large area, or growth in the heating system is a professional job.
  • Do not go on a roof for this checklist. Look from the ground and hire a roofer for the climb.

8. Move-out

Walk the home and compare it with the move-in notes and photos. Write the date. Ordinary wear is not the same thing as damage, and your state decides which costs can come out of a deposit.

Send the itemized accounting and any refund by the deadline in your state’s law. Because that deadline is not national, it is not printed as a number here. Keep a copy of what you sent and when.

Change locks if that is what your lease and local practice call for after you have legal possession again. Do not lock someone out to speed up a dispute. That can be illegal where you live.

9. Records

Keep, in a folder that is not only on one phone:

  • The lease and any written changes
  • The screening criteria and the consent for a report, if you used one
  • Lead disclosures and the pamphlet acknowledgment, when the federal rule applies
  • Move-in and move-out condition notes
  • Repair requests, what you did, and receipts
  • Insurance, permits, and inspection papers
  • Rent received and the deposit ledger

Tax treatment of rent, repairs, and improvements is its own subject. IRS Publication 527, Residential Rental Property, is the official publication. IRS recordkeeping guidance explains how long to keep records, and the answer depends on the record. This worksheet does not compute tax, depreciation, or a deductible loss.

What the lines mean

  • Collected rent = scheduled rent × (1 − vacancy). Other income is added after that.
  • Management = management percent × collected rent.
  • NOI = collected rent + other income − operating costs. Operating costs include management and exclude the loan, mortgage insurance, and the capital reserve.
  • Cash flow = NOI − principal and interest − mortgage insurance − capital reserve.
  • Cash invested = down payment + closing + rehab + other upfront cash. The borrowed amount is not included.
  • Cash-on-cash = annual cash flow ÷ cash invested. If you invested no cash, the ratio is left blank instead of dividing by zero.
  • A zero-interest payment is the loan divided by the number of months. Any other payment uses the standard amortizing formula: loan × (r × (1+r)n) ÷ ((1+r)n − 1), where r is the annual rate divided by 12 and n is years × 12.
  • Break-even scheduled rent is the rent that makes cash flow zero, with vacancy and management kept on that same basis. If those percentages leave no rent to put toward the bills, the sheet says the break-even rent is impossible. It still will not tell you what to charge.

When something breaks

Know the shutoffs before the emergency. Write locations here. The notes stay in this browser and are not sent anywhere.

Water

If a pipe is flooding and you can reach the main valve without stepping through water near electricity, turn that valve off and call a plumber. If water is around the electrical panel, do not stand in it and do not open the panel. Leave and call the utility.

Electricity

The main breaker turns the house off. Do not remove the panel cover or touch the meter. If you see sparks or smell burning, leave and call the fire department or the electric utility.

Gas and carbon monoxide

If you smell gas, or a CO alarm sounds, get everyone outside. CPSC says that if you suspect carbon monoxide poisoning, get to fresh air and then call 911. Do not look for a gas leak with a flame, and do not turn gas back on yourself. The utility or a licensed technician does that.

CPSC also says never to use a portable generator inside a home or garage, even with the doors open, and to keep a generator outside, at least 20 feet from the home, with the exhaust pointed away. Never use a gas oven to heat the house. Those are CPSC’s instructions, not a repair guide.

More general preparedness steps are on Ready.gov’s safety skills page.

No heat, or a sewage backup

Use only a heater the label allows, plugged into a wall outlet, and follow that label. Do not run a grill, camp stove, or generator indoors. For a sewage backup, stop using water and call a plumber. Do not open the sewer line as a project from this page.
